In Convex Position

Throw n random points into a triangle, a square, a disk. How often is every one of them a corner of the shape they span? Sylvester asked it for four points in 1864. It is exactly solved for a few bodies (and, for the disk, up to eight points), the “which body is best/worst” question is settled for four and five points and open from six on. This page tracks a campaign chipping at the six-point case. This page explains the problem, what is known, and how the campaign attacks it.

1 · The question

Every point a vertex

Take a convex body K in the plane (a triangle, a square, a disk — anything convex) and drop n points into it independently and uniformly at random. Stretch a rubber band around them: that is their convex hull. The points are in convex position when the band touches all n of them — no point is hiding inside. We write

PK(n) = P( n i.i.d. uniform points in K are in convex position )

For n = 3 this is always 1 (three points always form a triangle). For n = 4 the only way to fail is that one point lands inside the triangle of the other three — that is Sylvester’s four-point problem. For larger n, failure means the hull has fewer than n corners.

Two things make this tractable where the 3×3 eigenvalue problem was not. First, the event is affine-invariant: stretch, shear or rotate K and the probability does not change, so “square” really means every parallelogram and “disk” means every ellipse. Second, the underlying integrals live in 2n dimensions but factor through low-dimensional geometry (areas of triangles, lengths of chords), which is why exact answers exist at all.

P(6) is strictly increasing in m, from the triangle up to the disk (values also verified by direct 6×108-sample Monte Carlo) — the ordering the still-open n ≥ 6 extremal conjecture predicts, now on exact values. No single closed form in m exists (a genuine negative result: an 11-point fit to the P(5)-style shape does not resolve), and the leading large-m correction is of order 1/m4 with a constant that resisted identification even at 9 digits — recorded as unidentified rather than forced.

Novelty (2026 prior-art check). The exact P(6) for the pentagon, hexagon and octagon, the P(5) regular-polygon formula, the disk triangle-area moments (E[A3²], E[A3³]), the 4-point hull-area second moment E[A4²], and the covariance form of the P(5) identity were not found in the literature (Morin 2024 notes exact regular-polygon formulas “are rare”; MathWorld says the disk triangle-area distribution is “apparently not known exactly”) — the genuine new candidates. By contrast the triangle and square E[A3³] (31/9000, 137/72000) are already published (Reed 1974; Beck 2024), and E[A4] = 2E[A3] follows from classical Efron–Buchta identities; those are not claimed as new.

A second way to compute the same number

Write Ak for the area of the hull of k random points as a fraction of |K|. Because the vertex set of the hull of n points is some k-subset in convex position with the other n−k points inside it, the hull-vertex distribution is determined by hull-area moments, and unwinding that gives an alternating identity (Efron for n = 4, Buchta in general):

PK(n) = ∑j=0n−3 (−1)j C(n, j) E[ An−jj ]
e.g. P(4) = 1 − 4 E[A3], P(5) = 1 − 5 E[A4] + 10 E[A3²], P(6) = 1 − 6 E[A5] + 15 E[A4²] − 20 E[A3³]

This is our route M (src/route_moments.py): estimate the area moments with an exact Jarvis-march hull area — code that shares nothing with the direct convex-position testers — and combine. It reproduces all seven planar anchors, and every disk value we publish must agree between the direct route and route M before it is believed. It also says something structural: any body whose hull-area moments are known in closed form has PK(n) in closed form.

Day-one result · five points from four

Only two moments enter P(5) = 1 − 5E[A4] + 10E[A3²], and both are cheaper than they look. For any four points, twice the area of their hull equals the sum of the areas of the four triangles they span (both diagonal decompositions of a convex quadrilateral; or the outer triangle plus its three pieces), so E[A4] = 2E[A3] for every body and every i.i.d. law. And E[A3²] is a polynomial moment: (3/2)·det ΣK/|K|² with ΣK the covariance of the uniform law on K. Hence, for every planar convex body,

PK(5) = 1 − 10 E[A3(1 − A3)] = (5/2) PK(4) − 3/2 + 15 det ΣK/|K|²

Sylvester’s five-point problem reduces to his four-point problem plus the body’s inertia. It returns Valtr’s 11/36 and 49/144, gives Pdisk(5) = 1 − 305/(48π²) (which turned out to be in Marckert 2017 — a good check), a closed form for every regular polygon (table above), and half-disk 0.341070, stadium 0.352809. The identity is not in Marckert–Rahmani (arXiv:1511.03658 = Mathematika 2021, read in full: its engine is a 5-point “comb” recursion with no moment identities), and a 2026 prior-art check found it in no other source — a genuine novelty candidate, though only one step from classical facts.

Why Valtr’s formulas exist at all

For the square and triangle the probability is rational for every n — a striking fact. Valtr proved it by turning the geometry into counting: a convex polygon inside a square decomposes into four monotone chains, and the probability that random points form such chains reduces to counting lattice-path-like objects, giving squares of Catalan-type numbers. Nothing of the sort is available for the disk, whose boundary is not made of straight pieces; that is why the disk sits behind a recursion instead of a formula.

3 · Large n

How fast it dies

PK(n) decays faster than any exponential: roughly like n−2n. Bárány (1999) found the exact rate for every convex body:

limn→∞ n² · PK(n)1/n = e² A(K)³ / ( 4 |K|³ )

where A(K) is the affine perimeter of K — an affine-invariant length that is largest for ellipses and vanishes for polygons if measured naively, so for polygons Bárány’s theorem is really about the affine perimeter of the largest-affine-perimeter convex subset. The consequence: when points do land in convex position, their hull is with high probability close to a deterministic limit shape, the convex curve inside K of maximal affine perimeter. Hilhorst, Calka and Schehr (2008) sharpened the disk asymptotics to several more terms by mapping the problem onto a random-acceleration process. Both are covered, with exact statements, in LITERATURE.md.

For our purposes the lesson is practical: beyond n ≈ 12 plain Monte Carlo cannot see anything (P is already 10−5 for the square at n = 12), so “small n exactly, large n asymptotically” is the natural division of labour, and the interesting open questions live at small n.

4 · What is open

Three doors, one plan

Door A · the extremal conjecture — n = 4, 5 settled, n ≥ 6 open

Blaschke (1917) proved that among all convex bodies the triangle minimises and the ellipse maximises Sylvester’s four-point probability. The conjecture that Ptriangle(n) ≤ PK(n) ≤ Pellipse(n) for every n was proved for n = 5 by Marckert & Rahmani (Mathematika, 2021) and is open for n ≥ 6. Our identity gives a short independent proof of the n = 5 case: it says the conjecture is equivalent to F(K) = E[A3(1−A3)] being largest for triangles and smallest for ellipses, and a fibre-by-fibre computation with vertical sections shows that under Steiner symmetrisation and shaking the second moment changes by exactly the fraction b2/|K| < 1 of the change in the first — so F inherits Blaschke’s monotonicity, with exact gap formulas (verified to 9 digits on the half-disk). Details: docs/N5_PROOF.md. The n = 6 analogue needs E[A4²] and E[A3³] — genuinely new objects, and the real open door.

Door B · six points — the ingredients, solved

P(6) = 1 − 6E[A5] + 15E[A4²] − 20E[A3³]. All three moments now have first-principles routes: E[A3k] and E[A5] from a width-function integral, and E[A4²·1conv] from a two-chord integral built on hull area = ½|diagonal1 × diagonal2|. Together they give exact P(6) for every regular polygon (table above), matching Valtr at m = 3, 4 and Marckert’s disk in the limit — the first six-point values for polygons. What stays open is the extremal question at n = 6 (Marckert–Rahmani proved only n = 5): the exact values show P(6) rising monotonically from triangle to disk, consistent with the conjecture, but a proof needs the machinery pointed at general (non-regular) bodies.

Door C · three dimensions

In 3-D even Sylvester’s own problem (5 points) is known exactly for only three bodies (ball, cube, tetrahedron); n = 6 in the ball, and the extremal question (does the simplex minimise, the ball maximise, for all n?) are essentially open. The 5-point values are one-line consequences of expected-tetrahedron-volume results, so new bodies (octahedron, cylinder, regular prisms) are attackable with symbolic integration + high-precision quadrature.

5 · The campaign

How we attack it

  1. Pipeline first, then numbers. done Two independent testers (triangle/tetrahedron containment in numpy vs. a numba convex-hull count) must agree sample-for-sample; nine exact anchors reproduce at 2×107 samples on the server. Anything the pipeline says about an unknown value inherits this credibility.
  2. Map the small-n landscape. done Disk n = 5–10 at 2×108 samples (agrees with Marckert’s exact n = 5–8, |z| ≤ 1.6); exact P(5) for all regular m-gons; a 561-body scan of the n = 5 extremal window with zero violations; 3-D n = 5 anchors plus the new E[A5] = (5/2)E[A4] identity in R³.
  3. Reproduce a published exact method on a known case before pointing it at an unknown one. done Route P (exact line-space integration for polygons) reproduces Alikoski 1939 to 50 digits and Buchta’s 1984 expected-area table as exact rationals; the Efron/edge-count route reproduces Sylvester’s disk value to 10−41.
  4. The n = 6 front. ingredients solved All three moments now have first-principles routes (width integral for E[A3k], E[A5]; two-chord integral for E[A4²]), giving exact P(6) for every regular polygon — new values beyond Marckert’s disk. Next: point the machinery at non-regular bodies to attack the open n ≥ 6 extremal conjecture, and the Rd analogues.
  5. Same evidence bar as before. Nothing is called exact without two structurally independent confirmations; PSLQ hits count only when they come unprompted from many digits; every unverified claim stays labelled UNVERIFIED in the log.
  6. Deliverables. LITERATURE.md (multi-agent sweep with adversarial verification), TASK.md for the autonomous server session, PROGRESS.md timestamped log, results JSON per run, and eventually a write-up.
